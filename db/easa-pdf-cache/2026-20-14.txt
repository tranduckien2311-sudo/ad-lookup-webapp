SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION: Background The FAA issued a notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) to amend 14 CFR part 39 by adding an AD that would apply to certain The Boeing Company Model 737-600, -700, -700C, -800, -900, and -900ER series airplanes. The NPRM was published in the Federal Register on January 9, 2026 (91 FR 931); corrected January 16, 2026 (91 FR 2090). The NPRM was prompted by reports from multiple operators of pitch oscillation events due to excessive horizontal stabilizer freeplay. In the NPRM, the FAA proposed to require measuring the freeplay of the horizontal stabilizer pivot hinges and the jackscrew and applicable on-condition actions. The FAA is issuing this AD to address excessive horizontal stabilizer freeplay. The unsafe condition, if not addressed, could lead to a flutter event, which can result in loss of control of the airplane. Discussion of Final Airworthiness Directive Comments The FAA received comments from five commenters, including the Air Line Pilots Association, International, Boeing, and two individuals who supported the NPRM without change. The FAA received additional comments from four commenters, including Alaska Airlines, Aviation Partners Boeing (APB), Canadian North, and United Airlines. The following presents the comments received on the NPRM and the FAA's response to each comment. Request To Extend Certain Compliance Times Canadian North requested that the FAA extend the initial inspection threshold from 12 months to 36 months after the original issue date of Boeing Alert Requirements Bulletin 737-55A1104 RB, dated June 12, 2025 (Boeing Alert Requirements Bulletin 737-55A1104 RB), to align it with the repetitive inspection interval. The commenter stated this would also align the initial inspection with the typical heavy maintenance visit, which would allow sufficient time for procurement, planning, and logistical coordination, thereby minimizing the risk of unscheduled downtime and reducing administrative burden. United Airlines noted the repetitive inspection intervals are the same regardless of the inspection findings and applicable follow-on action, i.e., either keeping the original parts installed or replacing or repairing a worn part. The commenter stated it believes the repetitive inspection intervals for airplanes with newly replaced parts (specifically hinge bearings) should be longer than the intervals for airplanes that have original production parts installed. The FAA disagrees with extending the initial inspection threshold and the repetitive inspection intervals for airplanes with newly replaced parts. The FAA has determined that the compliance times identified in the required service information will ensure an acceptable level of safety for the fleet. Further, the repetitive inspection intervals were chosen to ensure that excessive freeplay does not exist for either original or new or replaced parts. However, under the provisions of paragraph (i) of this AD, the FAA will consider requests for approval of an extension of a compliance time, if sufficient data are submitted to substantiate that the new compliance time would provide an acceptable level of safety. The FAA has not changed this AD in this regard. Table 3, Condition 2 and Table 4, Condition 2 do not prohibit accomplishing an inspection earlier than the specified compliance time; therefore, operators may accomplish the interim inspection in Table 4, Condition 2 early, and then perform the repair or replacement as soon as practical within the same maintenance period. A post-repair/replacement inspection is still required to verify freeplay values D1, D2, D3, and H are within the Level 1 limits before the airplane is returned to service. Additionally, under the provisions of paragraph (i) of this AD, the FAA will consider AMOC requests for a deviation to the required actions, provided adequate supporting information is submitted. The FAA has not changed this AD in this regard. Request To Provide Credit for Other Service Information United Airlines requested that the FAA revise the proposed AD to provide credit for inspecting the horizontal stabilizer for free play using Boeing Multi-Operator Message (MOM) MOM-MOM-22-0187- 01B(R1), dated May 27, 2022 (Boeing MOM-MOM-22-0187-01B(R1)). The commenter noted that Boeing MOM-MOM-22-0187-01B(R1) states operators are able to obtain credit for performing this inspection once the eventual service bulletin and the potential AD are released. The commenter also stated it reviewed Boeing MOM-MOM-22-0187-01B(R1) and Boeing Alert Requirements Bulletin 737- 55A1104 RB and confirmed they contain identical work instructions for the freeplay inspection. The FAA disagrees because it is not necessary to provide credit in this AD. Airplanes on which accepted freeplay measurement procedures were accomplished using Boeing MOM-MOM-22-0187- 01B(R1), or other certain service information, are classified as Group 1, Configuration 1 airplanes. Refer to the note under paragraph 1.A., “Effectivity,” of Boeing Alert Requirements Bulletin 737- 55A1104 RB. The work instructions for Group 1, Configuration 1 airplanes already provide some relief for prior accomplishment of the freeplay measurement. The FAA has not changed this AD in this regard. Conclusion The FAA reviewed the relevant data, considered any comments received, and determined that air safety requires adopting this AD as proposed. Accordingly, the FAA is issuing this AD to address the unsafe condition on these products. Except for minor editorial changes, this AD is adopted as proposed in the NPRM. None of the changes will increase the economic burden on any operator. Authority for This Rulemaking Title 49 of the United States Code specifies the FAA's authority to issue rules on aviation safety. Subtitle I, section 106, describes the authority of the FAA Administrator. Subtitle VII: Aviation Programs, describes in more detail the scope of the Agency's authority. The FAA is issuing this rulemaking under the authority described in Subtitle VII, Part A, Subpart III, Section 44701: General requirements. Under that section, Congress charges the FAA with promoting safe flight of civil aircraft in air commerce by prescribing regulations for practices, methods, and procedures the Administrator finds necessary for safety in air commerce. This regulation is within the scope of that authority because it addresses an unsafe condition that is likely to exist or develop on products identified in this rulemaking action.
