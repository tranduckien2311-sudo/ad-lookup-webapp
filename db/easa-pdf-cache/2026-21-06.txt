SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION: Background The FAA issued a notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) to amend 14 CFR part 39 by adding an AD that would apply to all PW Model PW4052, PW4056, PW4060, PW4060A, PW4060C, PW4062, PW4062A, PW4152, PW4156, PW4156A, PW4158, PW4460, and PW4462 engines. The NPRM was published in the Federal Register on May 26, 2026 (91 FR 30514). The NPRM was prompted by multiple events involving PW PW4000-94 engines in which tailpipe fire, loss of thrust control, and engine in-flight shutdown occurred. A manufacturer investigation revealed that these events were caused by undetected deterioration of Pb sensors due to epoxy deterioration, which can lead to erroneous Pb sensor measurements and incorrect fuel commands. In the NPRM, the FAA proposed to require replacing Pb sensors within certain compliance times. The FAA is issuing this AD to address the unsafe condition on these products. Discussion of Final Airworthiness Directive Comments The FAA received comments from 10 commenters. Commenters included Air Line Pilots Association, International (ALPA), Atlas Air, Boeing Commercial Airplanes (Boeing), Delta Air Lines, Inc (DAL), European Air Transport Leipzig GmbH, FedEx, the Foundation for Aviation Safety, PW, United Airlines (United), and United Parcel Service (UPS). ALPA and Boeing supported the NPRM without change. The following presents the comments received on the NPRM and the FAA's response to each comment. Specifically, Atlas Air requested that the FAA include Collins Aerospace Service Bulletin (SB) EEC1-73-62 or PW SB PW4ENG 73-221 as approved methods of compliance. UPS requested that the FAA include replacement of Pb sensors with new Pb sensors (in accordance with the component maintenance manual or other approved procedures) and accomplishment of Collins Aerospace SB EEC1-73-62 as approved methods of compliance. Atlas Air noted that all required actions in the NPRM were derived from the accomplishment instructions in Collins Aerospace SB EEC1-73-62. The FAA disagrees with the request. Request To Include Additional Engine Models DAL and PW requested that the FAA revise paragraph (c) of the proposed AD to include engine models with a dash number suffix identified in PW SB PW4ENG 73-221, Revision No. 3. Specifically, DAL requested all dash number suffix variants, while PW requested only -3 (Phase III) models be included. PW noted that all -3 (Phase III) engine model configurations are included in both the FAA type certificate data sheet (TCDS) and PW SB PW4ENG 73-221, Revision No. 3. PW also stated that this revision would improve alignment between the NPRM and PW maintenance instructions and would eliminate ambiguity for operators during compliance planning. The FAA disagrees with the request because dash number suffixes and other configuration designations are not separate engine models recognized by the TCDS and therefore, are not listed in the applicability of this AD. The FAA notes that engines identified by dash number suffixes that correspond to listed TCDS engine models are already included within the applicability of this AD. The FAA did not change this AD as a result of this comment. Request To Limit Applicability to Engines With Certain Pb Sensors DAL requested that the FAA revise paragraph (c) of the proposed AD to only include engines with electronic engine controls (EEC) that have Pb sensor part number (P/N) 800833-1 or P/N 796163-1 installed. DAL stated that if Collins Aerospace certifies a new Pb sensor P/N, the certification process would ensure that the same deterioration, as addressed by the NPRM, would not exist. The FAA disagrees with the request. If a new configuration Pb sensor is released and proven not susceptible to the unsafe condition, then the FAA would consider adjusting the required actions for that Pb sensor at that time, including alternative methods of compliance (AMOC) requested under the provisions of paragraph (i) of this AD. The FAA did not change this AD as a result of this comment. Request To Clarify Tracking Method for Flight Hours (FH) DAL and United requested that the FAA revise paragraph (h) of the proposed AD to clarify that if the FH cannot be determined for the Pb sensor, guidance for determining the age of the Pb sensors may be found in PW SB PW4ENG 73-221, Revision No. 3. Specifically, United requested that guidance stating that the EEC operational hours can also be established by multiplying years in service by 3,048 hours or average utilization per year be included in the NPRM. United stated that industry experience indicates that while the EEC timer tracks sensor replacement intervals based on operational hours, a significant number of units arrive at shop visits with unreliable EEC times that cannot be used to determine operating hours. DAL noted that most operators track operating times (including time since new) and operating cycles (including cycles since new) of the EEC, similar to how operators track life limits on life limited parts, making EEC flight hour tracking a sufficient method for monitoring the 30,000 FH limit on the Pb sensor. The FAA disagrees with the request because operators are responsible for tracking and determining the FH and years since new or since repair for Pb sensors, and the methodology for this practice is not specifically required in this AD. Request To Clarify Use of “Years in Service” European Air Transport Leipzig GmbH requested that the FAA explain the use of the term “years in service” in the NPRM, and DAL requested that the FAA change the term “10 years in service” to “10 calendar years since the EEC was made serviceable with a new or repaired Pb sensor installed.” European Air Transport Leipzig GmbH stated that EECs stored in a controlled environment (individually or on preserved spare engines) do not experience epoxy deterioration in the same manner as EECs actively operated on aircraft, so the “years in service” counter should be applied differently for stored/preserved EECs versus those in active operation. DAL noted that the accomplishment instructions in PW SB PW4ENG 73-221, Revision No. 3 and Hamilton Sundstrand SB EEC131-73-62, Revision 1, dated January 20, 2023, determine Pb sensor operational hours and years of service based on EEC operating hours. DAL added that if the Pb sensor is not installed in an operating EEC, hours and years would not accrue, and time outside of an EEC would not count toward years of service. The FAA partially agrees with the request. The FAA agrees that the term “years in service” should be revised to provide clarity as to the intent of the compliance time, but the FAA disagrees with the proposed language. The FAA has revised the language in paragraphs (g) and (h) of this AD to “years since new or since last repair.” The FAA disagrees with commenter's position regarding epoxy deterioration on stored EECs because the FAA has no data demonstrating that EECs stored separately or installed on preserved spare engines experience a different rate of deterioration than EECs in active operation. However, the FAA notes that once this AD is published, any person may request approval of an AMOC under the provisions of paragraph (i) of this AD. 10/19/26, 9:30 AM 2026-21-06 Request To Clarify Compliance Time The Foundation for Aviation Safety requested that the FAA clarify why Pb sensor replacement is not an immediate compliance requirement in the NPRM. The Foundation for Aviation Safety stated that Pb sensor replacement is a 2-hour fix to guard against engine fires or other engine catastrophes. The FAA agrees to clarify. Although Pb sensor replacement is a maintenance task that can be accomplished in a relatively short amount of time, compliance time is not driven by ease of replacement, and the phased replacement approach required by this AD reduces operator burden while still ensuring the overall safety of the fleet.
